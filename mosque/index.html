Gelibolu Parade, Auburn · Western Sydney

Every day is an open day.

An Ottoman-style mosque built by Sydney's Turkish community and named for Gallipoli. A working house of prayer for 6,000 worshippers a week, and a landmark that welcomes more than 10,000 visitors a year, 95 per cent of them not Muslim.

Visit

Plan your visit

Everything a first-time visitor, a coach driver or a head teacher needs to know, published before they have to ring anyone.

What to wear

Modest clothing with shoulders and knees covered. Scarves and shoe covers are provided at the door, so nobody needs to bring anything or feel caught out.

How long it takes

A typical visit runs 45 to 60 minutes: the courtyard, the prayer hall, the İznik tilework, and time for questions with a guide.

Fridays

The two Jummah congregations run from 12:30pm to about 1:40pm and the building is given over to worship. Visits are not scheduled across that window; Friday slots sit either side of it and are shown on the booking page.

Getting here and parking

Gelibolu Parade, Auburn. The coach drop-off point, parking and the walk to the visitors' entrance are mapped on this page, so a teacher can plan the trip without a phone call.

Access

Step-free routes, seating for anyone who cannot sit on the floor, and accessible bathrooms are described here in full rather than left to be discovered on the day.

Photography

Photography is welcome in the courtyard and the hall outside prayer times. Please ask before photographing anyone at prayer.

6

What the current site never says. There is no dress code, duration, parking, accessibility or teacher information anywhere on gallipolimosque.org.au. The booking form contradicts itself on Friday tours, asks the booker's age and gender, and reveals a $5 per person charge only at the payment step.

Two ways to book a tour

Free, but ticketed, so the mosque knows exactly who is coming. Both channels run on Humanitix, which is already in use here.

Anyone, any month

The Gallipoli story

An Ottoman mosque in Sydney, named for Gallipoli.

Built by the Turkish-Australian community and named for the campaign where their grandfathers and ours faced each other. It is a reconciliation story with national resonance, and nobody has ever told it on this website.

It is the single strongest untold story in Australian faith tourism, and it belongs to Auburn if Auburn moves first. Its own page, written for visitors, teachers and journalists alike.

What the toggle really does

Press TR at the top of this page. The navigation, the prayer strip, the prayer names, the Jummah labels, the date and the headline all switch. A notice appears in Turkish explaining that the untranslated sections stay in English.

  • Turkish URLs of their own, so Google indexes them separately
